#+begin_abstract
Learned representations shape how models organize information and generalize
beyond their training examples. As learning systems evolve to incorporate
information from increasingly diverse modalities, a flexible and efficient
foundation for representation-learning research becomes essential to enable
progress. We introduce Representax, an open-source JAX framework for developing
and training representation-learning models across modalities. Representax
unifies extensible training and evaluation tasks with established methods
spanning dense and late-interaction retrieval, multimodal contrastive learning,
reward modeling, and joint-embedding predictive learning. Its shared training
infrastructure supports model adaptation, multimodal data processing,
memory-efficient optimization, and distributed execution on GPUs and TPUs. To
demonstrate its capabilities, we compare training performance against established
frameworks, train and evaluate retrieval and multimodal models, and measure
multi-GPU strong scaling. These experiments demonstrate competitive throughput,
improvements on held-out retrieval tasks, and efficient scaling. Representax
provides a foundation for investigating how representations are learned and
adapted, and for taking new ideas from exploration to scale.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:introduction
:END:
A recurring lesson in artificial intelligence is the power of general methods
that scale with computation over approaches built around hand-engineered
knowledge [cite:@sutton2019bitterlesson]. In representation learning, the emphasis
shifts from specifying representations by hand to developing methods that learn
them from observations of the world [cite:@bengio2013representation]. The research
challenge is to learn representations that both capture meaningful structure
in the world and support prediction, generation, and action. This development
spans early hierarchies of visual features and subsequent reconstruction-based
and predictive approaches to learning compact encodings, linguistic relationships,
and speech features
[cite:@fukushima1980neocognitron;@hinton2006dimensionality;@mikolov2013wordrepresentations;@baevski2020wav2vec].
Model architectures and learning signals shape not only what representations
capture, but how efficiently they are learned and how well they transfer to new
settings. In World Models, compressed visual observations and learned temporal dynamics
support a latent simulation in which an agent can learn a policy before
transferring it to the original environment [cite:@ha2018worldmodels]. Latent diffusion
makes image synthesis more computationally tractable by operating in a
learned latent space [cite:@rombach2022latentdiffusion]. In these systems,
representations provide more than features for a downstream task: they define
the spaces in which simulation, policy learning, and generation take place.

Multimodal learning extends this pursuit across different forms of observation.
CLIP learns image-text alignment jointly through contrastive supervision
[cite:@radford2021clip], while systems such as BLIP-2 connect separately pretrained visual
and language components [cite:@li2023blip2]. We suspect that future learning
systems will be natively multimodal throughout pretraining and post-training,
integrating information from a variety of data modalities. A longer-term
ambition is to learn unified representations of the world from these
different observations. [cite/t:@huh2024platonic] demonstrate increasing alignment
between visual and language representations as models scale, and propose the
Platonic Representation Hypothesis: that this convergence reflects progress
towards a shared statistical model of reality. Research in this direction examines how
representations are learned, aligned, and adapted, and when shared structure
supports transfer across tasks and modalities, prediction, and decision-making.

Progress towards this natively multimodal future requires software that enables researchers to
develop and test new ideas. Research in representation learning spans several
interdependent choices: model architectures, mechanisms for combining
modalities, learning objectives, relationships used as supervision, and
training recipes. Implementing a new idea should require changes that express
the method itself, with minimal additional code to integrate it into a complete
experiment. A framework for this research must support flexible experimentation,
efficient execution, and consistent evaluation against relevant benchmarks and
reference implementations.

We introduce Representax, a JAX framework for representation-learning research
towards natively multimodal models. The library is built on unified abstractions
for models, learning tasks, data, and evaluation. Together,
they connect method development to a shared training system while keeping
scientific decisions separate from execution strategies. Within supported
configurations, researchers can optimize execution for the available hardware
without coupling their model and objective definitions to a particular device
topology.

Representax extends the JAX ecosystem [cite:@jax], building on its composable
transformations and compiled GPU/TPU execution. It integrates Equinox for
native models [cite:@kidger2021equinox], Grain for data processing [cite:@grain],
Optax for optimization [cite:@optax], and Orbax for checkpointing [cite:@orbax].
For our initial implementation, we select established methods that provide
meaningful benchmarks and building blocks for further research: dense and
late-interaction retrieval, supervised similarity and ranking, reward modeling,
multimodal contrastive learning, masked language modeling, and joint-embedding
prediction.

We evaluate Representax through matched comparisons with established
implementations, end-to-end adaptation experiments, and fixed-work
multi-accelerator scaling. Our aim is to make ideas about how models represent
the world easier to turn into informative experiments, and those experiments
easier to build upon.

* Related Work
Representation-learning methods differ in the signals they learn from and how
they connect information across modalities. DINOv2 learns visual features that
capture semantic structure through self-supervision, without using labels or
captions as training targets [cite:@oquab2023dinov2]. Predictive methods such as
V-JEPA 2.1 learn spatial and temporal features from images and videos
[cite:@murlabadia2026vjepa]. Paired observations supply relationships across
modalities: early audiovisual learning and image-sentence alignment
[cite:@ngiam2011multimodal;@karpathy2015alignments] precede the large-scale
contrastive approaches of CLIP and ImageBind
[cite:@radford2021clip;@girdhar2023imagebind]. Combining modalities within a
model introduces further architectural choices. BLIP-2 and Idefics3 connect
pretrained visual and language components
[cite:@li2023blip2;@laurencon2024idefics3], while Chameleon studies early fusion
of image and text tokens [cite:@chameleon2024]. Qwen3-Omni extends multimodal
modeling to text, image, audio, and video perception, with text and speech
generation [cite:@xu2025qwen3omni]. These approaches locate cross-modal
learning in different places: the supervision, the connections between
components, and the representations learned jointly.

Language models trained on text have learned representations from which
geographical coordinates and historical dates can be recovered with linear
probes, generalizing to entities held out from probe training
[cite:@gurnee2024spacetime]. Such evidence of learned world structure
complements work connecting independently trained models. A learned linear
projection from a frozen visual encoder into a frozen language model supports
image captioning and visual question answering [cite:@merullo2023linearly].
Increasing alignment between larger vision and language models motivates the
Platonic Representation Hypothesis of a shared statistical model of reality
[cite:@huh2024platonic]. These findings make the route towards shared
representations a concrete research problem: which learning signals, data
relationships, and architectures encourage useful common structure? The
relationship between alignment and performance depends on the shared and
modality-specific information required by a task
[cite:@tjandrasuwita2025alignment], motivating evaluation of both cross-modal
transfer and retained capabilities.

Developing such methods involves choices about representational granularity,
adaptation, and the cost of learning. ColBERT retains token-level embeddings
for late-interaction retrieval [cite:@khattab2020colbert], while Matryoshka
objectives train useful representations at multiple embedding dimensions
[cite:@kusupati2022matryoshka]. LoRA constrains parameter updates through
trainable low-rank matrices [cite:@hu2021lora], and gradient caching separates
contrastive batch size from encoder activation memory [cite:@gao2021gradcache].
These choices can be composed in the training recipes evaluated here.
Multimodal adaptation also raises the question of what to preserve. LiT keeps a
pretrained image tower fixed while tuning a text tower to align with it
[cite:@zhai2022lit];
jina-embeddings-v5-omni trains connectors between frozen encoders while
preserving its text embeddings [cite:@honicke2026jina]. Our adaptation study
compares connector-only, LoRA, and full-parameter recipes through media
retrieval and text-retrieval retention. Beyond retrieval, promptable
vision-language representations use task context to support reinforcement
learning [cite:@chen2024promptable], illustrating how the use of a
representation can itself become a research choice. Learned representations
can also supply objectives for search: [cite/t:@tian2021esclip] use CLIP
similarity to guide evolutionary optimization of triangle-based drawings.

Research libraries make these choices accessible through reusable
implementations and experimental workflows. Scenic supports vision and
multimodal research with distributed JAX training [cite:@dehghani2022scenic],
and solo-learn provides shared training and evaluation infrastructure for
self-supervised visual learning [cite:@dacosta2022sololearn]. LAVIS provides
unified interfaces for language-vision models, datasets, training, and
evaluation [cite:@li2023lavis]. Sentence Transformers supports embedding training and retrieval
[cite:@reimers2019sentencebert], while PyLate extends this ecosystem with
training and indexing for late-interaction models [cite:@chaffin2025pylate].
Representax provides a shared JAX foundation for developing, composing, and
evaluating representation-learning methods across modalities. Its interfaces
connect model and objective changes to shared data processing, training, and
evaluation, allowing research methods to build on a common implementation.

* Representax
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:system
:END:
Research code should isolate the changes under investigation, reusing efficient,
validated implementations for the rest of the system. Representax makes the
model, learning procedure, data policy, and execution settings explicit, so
researchers can inspect and revise the experiment they are running. Our vision
for Representax is to make implementing a new idea require only the changes
needed to express it, with minimal additional code to train, evaluate, and build
upon the result.

** Core Abstractions
Four abstractions are core to experiments in representation learning: models
and processors, learning tasks, data distributions, and evaluators. Efficient
shared infrastructure connects these components into complete experiments
(Figure [[fig:architecture]]).

- *Models and processors.* We implement models as Equinox modules, which are
  JAX PyTrees with parameters and submodules stored in fields and forward
  computations defined by Python methods [cite:@kidger2021equinox]. This keeps
  model structure and computation available for inspection and modification.
  Associated processors prepare inputs according to each model's supported
  modalities and processing requirements. The choice of how to update a model
  is similarly explicit: parameter selections follow its PyTree structure,
  allowing researchers to train selected components, introduce LoRA-based
  adaptation [cite:@hu2021lora], or update the full model. Architectural changes
  that preserve the model and task interfaces can reuse the same training loop.
- *Learning tasks.* A learning task defines how examples and model outputs
  contribute to the training objective. Changing that objective changes the
  optimization problem, while the code that applies the selected optimizer,
  runs evaluations, and manages checkpoints can remain shared. Some tasks expose
  additional structure that supports composition: our Matryoshka implementation
  evaluates an objective at several embedding dimensions while reusing one
  encoder pass [cite:@kusupati2022matryoshka], and predictive tasks can specify
  updates to a target encoder. Keeping these behaviors with the task permits
  method-specific extensions while preserving useful code reuse.
- *Data distributions.* Data selection is an experimental choice, expressed
  through source definitions, example mappings, and replaceable sampling
  pipelines. Weighted mixtures are one built-in selection policy;
  caller-provided Grain datasets and iterators permit other policies to use the
  same loading and processing interfaces.
- *Evaluators.* Evaluators connect a learning method to established measures
  of representation quality. Fourteen implementations cover retrieval,
  similarity, classification, clustering, reward modeling, and predictive
  representation learning, among other tasks. Compatible evaluators can be
  composed over a shared batch stream, collecting multiple measurements in one
  traversal. Dataset adapters connect these implementations to BEIR-format
  retrieval data and pinned sources including Sprint Duplicate Questions,
  Banking77, Twenty Newsgroups, and CIFAR-100. Shared processing and metric
  implementations support comparisons under a consistent protocol, both during
  training and in standalone evaluation.

** Source-Backed Data
Representax constructs training inputs from declared data sources without
requiring a second, framework-specific dataset. Source adapters provide access
to records; example mappings define their interpretation for a learning task;
model-associated processors prepare the relevant model inputs. Changing a data
source mixture can therefore reuse the same processing and training code.
Each stage has a defined role, keeping data selection and processing explicit
while sharing efficient input execution.

For efficiency, supported pipelines read records and decode media on demand,
with bounded prefetch overlapping input preparation and accelerator computation.
Built-in adapters cover local datasets and revision-pinned Hugging Face sources,
while referenced media can be fetched as needed from local or HTTP(S) locations.
Remote media references need not be fully staged on the host before training;
custom readers and Grain iterators provide extension points for other remote
or streaming sources. Examples can be interleaved across sources or drawn as
source-local batches, according to the task's collation and model-processing
requirements.

Reproducibility depends on the data policy as well as the model. With fixed
source contents and deterministic processing, supported data loaders restore
saved sampling and iterator state directly, avoiding replay of previously
consumed batches. Caller-defined Grain datasets and iterators also provide an
extension point for alternative sampling and curation methods. Future research
investigating learner-dependent data selection and curation can build upon
this interface.

** Configuring Experiments
A job configuration assembles the model, learning task, data distributions,
and evaluators together with the optimizer, training schedule, and artifact
settings. In Representax, configurations are typed Python objects with
immutable fields and runtime validation through Pydantic. Hydra-Zen supports
their composition and launch without requiring YAML. Experimental recipes can
therefore live alongside their implementations as reviewable code, while each
run records the resolved configuration used to construct it.

Within this configuration system, Representax distinguishes *scientific
parameters*, which define the learning problem, from *execution parameters*,
which describe how supported computations are carried out. Model and objective
choices, data sampling, trainable parameters, and optimization schedules belong
to the former; device placement, encoder microbatch sizes, and prefetch settings
belong to the latter. The run record retains the complete configuration together
with its scientific and execution components, making both the experimental
recipe and its hardware-specific realization explicit.

The configured job enters a shared lifecycle covering training, scheduled
evaluation, checkpointing, and inference export. Checkpoints preserve model
and optimizer state, random state, training progress, and the data iterator
position. Resume checks compare the recorded scientific configuration and data
contract, distinguishing continuation of a run from changes to its method.
Together with pinned code, data, and environments, these records provide the
basis for reproducing an experiment and extending it through explicit changes.

** Efficient Execution
Representax leverages JAX's flexible device meshes and array sharding to specify
how computation and data are placed across accelerators. Training and evaluation
operate on these model and batch arrays. For convenience, we provide DDP- and
FSDP-style training configurations, while custom layouts can be expressed
through explicit parameter partition rules and mesh definitions. These choices
expose the tradeoffs between memory capacity and communication for a given
workload and compute topology.

Execution strategies also exploit the structure of particular learning
objectives. For supported contrastive tasks, Representax implements gradient
caching to separate encoder activation memory from the configured contrastive
batch [cite:@gao2021gradcache]. In our multiple-negatives ranking (MNR)
implementation [cite:@henderson2017smartreply], *encoder microbatches* are subsets
processed during encoding and gradient replay; the loss still compares
representations over the configured negative pool. This is an example of a
task-specific execution path, with compatibility checks and tests against direct
loss, gradient, and optimizer-update computations.

For objectives that decompose over examples or tokens, ordinary gradient
accumulation provides a separate microbatching strategy. Both paths reuse the
shared training lifecycle, while their applicability follows the structure of
the task. This allows established execution techniques to support new
experiments without treating every objective as computationally interchangeable.

* Framework and Accelerator Comparisons
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:comparisons
:END:
We first ask whether a common research framework can execute established
workloads at practical training rates. This comparison measures execution,
not the quality of newly learned representations; Section [[#sec:learning]]
addresses learning separately.

** Matched-Work Protocol
Thirteen recipes run on a single RTX 4090 and on a 16-chip v5e TPU slice,
with five repetitions per framework and recipe (seeds 7, 42, 773, 1234, and
2026). Each panel contains 130 runs; five further GPU runs provide a dense
TorchInductor control. Some repetitions retain deterministic data order,
so seeds describe execution variation, not independent learning experiments.

Within each hardware/recipe pair, we match examples, global batch, objectives,
and precision policies, while retaining framework-native execution.
The references are documented integrations with maintained PyTorch-based
libraries [cite:@paszke2019pytorch], not claims of each library's best possible
configuration. GPU references use eager execution in the main table;
the matched compiled dense control is reported in Appendix [[#sec:compiled-reference]].
GPU and TPU batch sizes can differ; ratios compare frameworks on the same
allocation, not accelerator types against one another.

For run \(r\), throughput is \(R_r=\sum_t B_t/\sum_t T_t\), where \(T_t\)
covers a complete optimizer-step interval, including visible input wait,
replay, synchronization, and optimization. We select up to 20 final warm
updates and exclude first-use and checkpoint-contaminated intervals.
Compilation, initialization, evaluation, and checkpointing are accounted for
separately where recorded. Appendix [[#sec:paired-methods]] documents workloads,
corrections, timing exclusions, and per-seed variation.

** Results and Scope
Table \ref{tab:framework-throughput} reports median rates, not a task-averaged
score. Representax improves throughput in many tested workloads, including
audio/video contrastive learning and reward modeling, but the exceptions matter.
GPU dense retrieval reaches 305.35 examples/s against eager Sentence
Transformers' 211.22 (1.446x), yet remains below the compiled control's 316.61.
BERT Base sentence-pair controls are slower on GPU, and TPU dense retrieval is
approximately tied. The outcome therefore depends on the workload and backend,
rather than on a universal framework ordering.

* End-to-End Representation Learning
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:learning
:END:
Efficient execution is useful only if complete experiments can reveal how
representations change. We therefore evaluate adaptation on held-out retrieval
tasks using normalized discounted cumulative gain at rank ten (nDCG@10).
We report seeds 7, 42, and 773 at the final scheduled checkpoint, rather
than selecting the best evaluation score. These experiments establish
finite-budget learning, not asymptotic or reference-matched convergence.

** Learning and Transfer
Dense adaptation trains ETTIN Encoder 150M on one presentation of 488,576
duplicate-free MS MARCO pairs [cite:@ettin;@bajaj2016msmarco].
NanoMSMARCO nDCG@10 rises from 0.0730 to 0.5794. Evaluation against the full
TREC DL 2019 and Natural Questions corpora also improves, from 0.0170 to
0.5404 and from 0.0032 to 0.2922, respectively
[cite:@craswell2020trec;@kwiatkowski2019nq]. Each comparison uses the same corpus
and scoring settings before and after adaptation.

CLIP ViT-B/32 trains on 588,800 COCO image-caption pairs, exposing five distinct
captions per image over the run without repeating an image within a batch
[cite:@radford2021clip;@lin2014coco]. Both text-to-image and image-to-text
retrieval improve on a held-out 1,000-image Flickr30k panel
[cite:@young2014flickr]. Table \ref{tab:learning} summarizes the results.
These are adaptation experiments, not reproductions of the original pretraining
regimes. The same infrastructure also exposes an unfavorable result:
late-interaction adaptation reduces training loss but regresses from
0.7104 to 0.6925 nDCG@10 on NanoMSMARCO
(Appendix [[#sec:learning-details]]).

** Multimodal Adaptation and Retention
We next use Jina v5 Omni Nano to study how adapting modality pathways affects
retrieval [cite:@honicke2026jina]. Three recipes train connectors and selected
delimiter embeddings, those parameters plus rank-eight LoRA, or the full model.
Each runs 2,000 updates at batch 32 with symmetric contrastive learning and
Matryoshka dimensions 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, and 768.

Connector-only training samples image-text, audio-text, and video-text sources.
The other recipes also sample text-text pairs. Learning rates differ
(\(2\times10^{-4}\), \(5\times10^{-5}\), and \(10^{-5}\), respectively).
This is a comparison of complete adaptation recipes, not a causal ablation of
parameter selection: data mixtures and optimization also change.
All retrieval directions are text-anchored; no unseen modality-to-modality
transfer is evaluated.

* Scaling and Sharding
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:scaling
:END:
The third study asks whether execution scales when the learning problem is
held fixed. A controlled 505-million-parameter ModernBERT masked-language
model runs on 1, 2, 4, and 8 A100 SXM4 40-GB GPUs
[cite:@warner2024modernbert;@devlin2019bert]. Global batch remains 256
sequences of length 512 (131,072 tokens per update). Local microbatch 16 and
16/8/4/2 accumulation rounds preserve that batch across device counts.
Parameter-gradient synchronization follows accumulation.

Three seeds each run 21 updates, with 20 measured after first use. Within each
seed, token and masking hashes match across device counts, and all runs use
BF16 compute, FP32 parameters and optimizer state, and full rematerialization.
The model is randomly initialized and the text repeated from WikiText-2
[cite:@merity2016wikitext]; this is strong scaling of a fixed workload, not a
complete pretraining study or a quality comparison.

* Design Analysis
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:design
:END:
The separation of scientific and execution choices is useful only when it helps
explain measured behavior. Earlier dense-retrieval diagnostics with ETTIN
Encoder 150M illustrate why a framework comparison must account for both the
learning problem and the work executed. They are short, single-seed systems
studies, separate from the replicated panel in Section [[#sec:comparisons]];
their duplicate-heavy data support systems diagnostics, not conclusions about
representation quality.

In a 30-update padding study, coarse native buckets reach 361.08 examples/s
against eager Sentence Transformers' 391.69. Forcing the reference to the same
padded shapes reduces its rate to 346.35. Finer native buckets reach 393.16,
while the native-padding compiled reference remains faster at 667.90.
Thus padding explains the eager crossover, but not the remaining compiled
reference advantage. Smaller padded shapes are not automatically better
overall: they also alter the compiled signatures that must be prepared.

The execution schedule matters independently of the objective. In the same
diagnostic program, the custom-VJP path reaches 361.08 examples/s against
341.31 for rematerialized GradCache. Subsequent layout and static-execution
changes reach 618.67 examples/s; these cumulative changes are not a single
factor ablation. A fresh process loads the persistent cache, reducing
compilation plus first execution from 287.51 to 21.60 seconds while reproducing
620.02 examples/s. Appendix [[#sec:design-details]] preserves these measurements
and their boundaries. The design lesson is to expose execution choices and
measure their effects, rather than assume that one configuration is optimal
for every workload.

* Capabilities, Limitations, and Reproducibility
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:limits
:END:
The evidence supports a research framework spanning multiple objectives and
modalities, useful held-out adaptation, and GPU/TPU execution. It does not
establish state-of-the-art representation quality, universal speedups,
production reliability, or performance across all implemented combinations.
Appendix [[#sec:capabilities]] separates implemented capabilities from evaluated
configurations. In particular, the TPU panel uses four worker VMs, but the
GPU scaling study stays within one node.

Training rates depend on data preparation, compilation, and timing boundaries;
short windows do not characterize long-running failures or every cache state.
Retrieval studies cover selected benchmarks, and held-out splits cannot rule
out upstream pretraining contamination. NanoMSMARCO contains only 50 queries.
The multimodal comparison changes complete recipes, so it cannot isolate
whether text degradation arises from parameter updates, sampling, or learning
rate.

The frozen evidence retains per-run measurements, source revisions, data
manifests, and environment records. Figures and numerical tables are regenerated
locally from those records without model loading or new training. Numbered
experiment launchers specify the original recipes; Appendix [[#sec:reproduction]]
documents reconstruction and provenance. New research should extend that
record: adaptive data, online learning, and representation learning through
interaction require their own protocols, evaluation, and state-management
contracts, not merely another loss function.

* Conclusion
Representax provides a shared foundation for representation-learning research,
connecting flexible method development with the infrastructure needed to train,
evaluate, and scale experiments. Its separation of scientific choices from
execution strategies allows researchers to investigate learning methods while
adapting supported workloads to available hardware. Our evaluations demonstrate
competitive training throughput across a range of GPU and TPU workloads,
improvements in held-out retrieval through adaptation, and efficient fixed-work
scaling to eight accelerators.
This separation also opens a path to hardware-aware autotuning, selecting
execution configurations for a fixed training recipe and compute environment
while preserving the intended learning computation.

These results provide a starting point for research towards natively multimodal
systems. Our adaptation studies motivate a concrete challenge: jointly improving
representations across modalities while preserving or strengthening existing
text capabilities, rather than relying on a frozen text pathway. Beyond fixed
training recipes, future work could investigate online representation learning,
reinforcement learning that shapes representations through interaction,
evolutionary search over architectures and learning objectives, and adaptive
datasets whose composition, sampling, or generation changes with the learner.
Indeed, some of these approaches may draw
inspiration from biological systems, informing how models integrate sensory
information, learn from experience, and adapt to changing environments. We intend
Representax to make these ideas practical to develop, compare, and refine.
With the community, we aim to build on this foundation to
investigate a shared question: how should models represent and understand the
world?

*Text tasks.* Dense retrieval uses the pinned Sentence Transformers MPNet
checkpoint and a 65,536-pair duplicate-free MS MARCO table, with seed-specific
permutations. Its native encoder chunks are 32; the GPU reference uses 128.
The TPU reference uses local batches of 128 and local negative pools.
Similarity uses STS Benchmark pairs and cosine regression; classification
uses a bounded duplicate-question collection and a cosine contrastive
objective with margin 0.5. Cross-encoder training uses binary labels from
3,840 pairs involving 1,920 queries. Historical late interaction uses
15,360 source-order pairs with repeated query-positive examples, temperature
0.02, and native chunk eight. It must not be conflated with the revised
hard-negative learning experiment.

*Reward tasks.* Outcome reward uses Bradley--Terry preference training on
3,840 UltraFeedback rows. Process reward uses binary process labels from
Math-Shepherd, with examples selected for four steps and mixed labels.
The latter's preparation bound is 256 tokens. Both frameworks execute padded
length 256 on GPU and 2,048 on TPU. The original GPU reference incorrectly
used 2,048 while native execution used 256; the corrected reference rerun
retains the same data, checkpoint, five seeds, optimizer, and batch settings.
Its actual training tensors are checked as microbatch two by length 256.
The replacement process-reward panel also shares the initial one-row scalar
head. GPU timing uses identical update indices in both frameworks, excluding
1, 2, and the post-checkpoint update 12; TPU timing excludes 1 and 2.
Original measurements remain in the artifact correction history, not the
active throughput table.
GPU native accumulation uses microbatches four and two,
respectively, to reach the stated global batches. Both use peak learning
rate \(10^{-5}\). Historical outcome reward loaded a BF16 base in both
frameworks, but only native initialization promoted trainable leaves to FP32
before creating optimizer state. The replacement GPU control promotes the
same BF16-rounded base and shared scalar head to FP32 in both frameworks;
forward computation remains BF16. Each framework uses microbatch four and
32 accumulation rounds for global batch 128. The independently initialized
historical heads and BF16 reference optimizer are not the active GPU comparison.
The corrected TPU control uses the same precision and shared-head policy,
with global batch 128. Its reference uses microbatch two and four local
accumulation rounds; native execution retains its recorded configuration.
The TPU reference averages parameter gradients before clipping and checks
full trainable-parameter identity across ranks outside measured timing.
Historical process-reward reference construction preceded Trainer seeding and
selected one score from a newly initialized classification head without an
explicit one-label configuration. The replacement controls correct both issues.

*Multimodal tasks.* Image-text uses 3,840 COCO images with four repeated
presentations of the first caption per image in the historical timing
recipe. The longer CLIP study instead uses distinct captions. Audio-text
uses 1,920 AudioCaps clips, repeated four times; video-text uses 3,840
MSR-VTT videos. Both adapt rank-four, alpha-eight text-tower LoRA in the
3B LCO model, with the base in BF16. Both corrected GPU panels use encoder
chunks of one. Historical native TPU runs used chunks of two; the corrected
TPU settings are detailed below. TPU reference execution uses direct MNR.
These are heavier multimodal workloads than text-only
encoding, not an equal-compute comparison across modalities.
Native trainable adapters are FP32, whereas the historical Transformers
[cite:@wolf2020transformers] =add_adapter= path leaves them BF16. Native A matrices use a normal
distribution with standard deviation \(d_{in}^{-1/2}\); PEFT defaults to a
uniform distribution on \([-d_{in}^{-1/2},d_{in}^{-1/2}]\). Both initialize
B to zero, but this does not align subsequent optimization. The historical
reference also constructs these adapters before Trainer applies its seed.

For historical TPU audio specifically, batch 48 gives three examples per chip. Native
and reference runs both use device-local negative pools: each audio query
is compared against three captions, not all 48 captions in the optimizer
batch. We matched the reference runner's configured objective; its existing
PJRT integration did not connect Sentence Transformers' cross-device
embedding gather. This is a limitation of that integration, not of the
Sentence Transformers MNR objective or its GPU distributed support. Gradient
synchronization alone does not expand the contrastive candidate pool.

Native rematerialized GradCache uses chunks of two, padding each device's encoder
input to four slots and discarding the extra representation before the loss.
The reference directly encodes its three examples. Both enable layer
activation checkpointing; native execution additionally rematerializes the
encoder chunks. These are execution differences, not a change in the number
of examples or negatives contributing to the objective. All five historical
native runs are slower. Padding and replay are plausible contributors, but no controlled
direct-versus-cached TPU audio ablation isolates their effect on throughput.

The corrected audio panel uses direct MNR in both frameworks with all 48
captions as candidates: each reference rank scores its three queries against
the complete gathered pool. Both use FP32 adapters and optimizer moments, the
same initialization distribution, and zero B matrices. Native and reference
median rates are 11.24 and 8.50 examples/s. This replacement changes the
negative pool and execution together; it does not isolate the historical
padding overhead. All five reference runs pass final sixteen-rank adapter
identity checks.

The corrected TPU video panel retains global batch 112 and device-local pools
of seven in both frameworks. Native GradCache uses chunk seven without padding;
the reference directly encodes seven examples per rank. Both retain layer
activation checkpointing and use FP32 adapters and optimizer moments. All five
reference runs pass final sixteen-rank parameter identity. Reference seeds
773, 1234, and 2026 ran on a replacement allocation in =us-west1-c=; the other
corrected video cells ran in =us-central1-a=. Both allocations use sixteen v5e
chips in a 4x4 topology and the same locked training environments. This is a
same-configuration comparison across documented allocations, not a claim that
all cells occupied the same physical slice.

*Predictive video.* V-JEPA uses a 12-layer, width-768 ViT-B/16 encoder,
12-layer width-384 predictor, tubelet size two, and supervision at layers
2, 5, 8, and 11. The EMA coefficient is 0.99925. The initialization and
short schedule define an execution test, not a released checkpoint's quality
evaluation. GPU and TPU use 24 and 2,816 Something-Something-v2 training
videos, respectively. Per-run manifests establish this difference; a later
shared asset directory is not authoritative for an earlier run.

Except for the noted reward and predictive-video schedules, paired tasks
use AdamW with peak learning rate \(2\times10^{-5}\), zero weight decay, and
gradient clipping at one; late interaction uses \(3\times10^{-6}\).
Most use one warmup update followed by cosine decay across 22 updates.
V-JEPA instead uses weight decay 0.04, no clipping, and a linear
\(10^{-4}\) to \(6\times10^{-4}\) schedule over 12,000 updates.
Native matrix computation is BF16 with FP32 accumulation in the recorded
precision policy. Frozen multimodal bases and outcome-reward loading have
the exceptions described above.

** Reference Execution and Environments
Sentence Transformers supplies dense, sentence-pair, cross-encoder, and
contrastive multimodal reference paths. PyLate supplies the late-interaction
path. Reward adapters use the Torch/TRL ecosystem [cite:@vonwerra2020trl]; predictive video uses
the official V-JEPA implementation. Launch, data, and metric adapters make
these programs fit the shared measurement contract. Thus /reference/
means the documented upstream-based integration, not that every upstream
training loop was used unchanged.

GPU comparisons use PyTorch eager unless explicitly marked TorchInductor.
TPU references use PyTorch/XLA 2.9.0 and PJRT. The same program launches
on all four worker VMs, yielding sixteen ranks. Native JAX uses four
distributed processes, each controlling four local TPU devices. This is
observed multi-host execution, not multi-node GPU scaling. Sentence
Transformers' cross-device-negative helper was not connected to an initialized
Torch distributed group in the historical integration; its paired contrastive
recipes generally used per-device negatives, with the late-interaction mismatch
identified above. The corrected late-interaction reference instead uses a
global pool through layout-pinned XLA collectives. Its gather passes real-tensor
identity and normalization checks on all sixteen ranks; its backward reduction
includes remote-query contributions and passes a same-backend global-batch
gradient/update oracle. The earlier unpinned reference fails the real-tensor
check and is not used for the corrected table. Corrected audio initializes the
Torch distributed group and uses Sentence Transformers' global embedding gather;
its observed score matrix is three by 48 on each rank. The corrected
TPU reference integration explicitly averages functional gradient outputs
before clipping and updating, checks replica identity, and requests BF16
autocast without downcasting FP32 master weights or optimizer state. Its
post-backward execution boundary is included in measured step time. These
are implementation-level comparisons, not isolated compiler benchmarks.

The native environment records JAX/jaxlib 0.11.1, Equinox 0.13.8,
Grain 0.2.18, Optax 0.2.8, and Orbax 0.12.4. GPU references record
PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128; TPU references record PyTorch 2.9.0+cpu and
Torch/XLA 2.9.0. Sentence Transformers is 5.6.1 in the principal environment;
the PyLate environment uses 5.3.0, alongside PyLate 1.6.0. Transformers
versions differ between these environments. Rather than assigning one
invented common version, the supplementary inventory preserves each
run's complete environment.

** Pairing Audit and Short-Run Checks
The original late-interaction comparison [L] used duplicated pairs with
sequential native batches but shuffled GPU reference batches. Its cached
reference backpropagated summed rather than mean gradients. Native TPU
execution also ignored the configured local-negative scope, comparing 512
candidates against the reference's 32. The corrected GPU panel uses shared
duplicate-free data, sequential batches, 32/256-token limits, and mean-loss
cached gradients. Initial NanoMSMARCO nDCG@10 is 0.7123 versus 0.7089;
mean final scores are 0.7151 versus 0.7089. GPU reference trajectories are
identical across seeds under this deterministic schedule. Both corrected
TPU frameworks use global pools of 512; final losses are 0.02067 and 0.02254,
with deterministic histories within each framework.

Outcome reward [O] aligns FP32 master weights and moments as described above.
Final paired losses differ by at most 0.0081 on GPU and 0.0057 on TPU.
Process reward [I] aligns scalar-head initialization and applies seeds before
model construction. Every corrected TPU reference verifies identical initial
and final trainable parameters across all sixteen ranks. These checks support
paired execution, not equivalence of long-run learning.

Corrected audio/video adapters [M] use FP32 trainable parameters and moments,
seeded normal A matrices at the same scale, and zero B matrices. The random
A draws are independent across frameworks, not bitwise shared. Final paired
TPU losses differ by at most 0.0544 for audio and 0.0055 for video. Audio's
replacement changes both the negative pool and execution method; video's
native chunk seven removes padding while preserving seven local candidates.
Neither comparison isolates a single source of performance improvement.

Two input-cache qualifications [C] remain: TPU image-text and V-JEPA show
first-seed cold-filesystem effects, and reference pre-materialization moves
some preprocessing outside the training timer. The V-JEPA GPU/TPU batch and
data differences [G] are recorded above. Process reward's short valid inputs
[P], even when padded to 2,048 on TPU, do not represent long-reasoning training.

:CUSTOM_ID: sec:scaling-details

#+INCLUDE: "tables/scaling.org"

Speedup is \(S_{n,s}=R_{n,s}/R_{1,s}\) for device count \(n\) and seed \(s\);
efficiency is \(E_{n,s}=S_{n,s}/n\). The reported speedup averages within-seed
ratios, not the ratio of independently averaged rates. Compiled executable
memory is approximately 32.5--33.6 GiB per device. Cold compilation takes
roughly 126--148 seconds, versus about 14 seconds for cached executable loading.
These startup costs are separate from warm throughput.

The controlled ModernBERT encoder uses 32 layers, width 1,280,
intermediate width 2,048, twenty attention heads, and vocabulary size
32,768. Global attention occurs every third layer; local attention
uses window 128. Global/local RoPE bases are 160,000/10,000 and layer
normalization epsilon is \(10^{-5}\). The MLM head uses exact GELU.
Tokenization uses BERT Base Uncased; the larger configured output vocabulary
is part of this controlled capacity workload, not a claim of a standard
released model.

WikiText-2 supplies 23,767 nonempty rows and 2,327,462 corpus tokens.
Text is repeated as needed to supply 2,752,512 tokens for 21 updates.
The masking procedure selects 15% of eligible tokens with the standard
80/10/10 mask/random/unchanged rule. The random replacement domain is
the valid tokenizer vocabulary. This small real-text corpus is adequate
for a compute workload but not evidence of general-purpose pretraining.

AdamW uses learning rate \(10^{-4}\), weight decay 0.01, and clipping
at one. Timing includes input preparation and device placement but not
offline tokenization. The first update is excluded from warm rates.
Memory is derived from the compiled executable's arguments, outputs,
temporary buffers, and aliasing, rather than equating JAX's reserved
allocator pool with live model memory.

A prior six-RTX-4090 workstation diagnostic showed substantial exposed
gradient-collective time. It motivated measurement on the A100 SXM4 node.
This is not a controlled NVLink ablation: processors, hosts, and selected
microbatches also differ. The paper therefore reports the A100 strong-scaling
experiment on its own terms and does not assign all hardware differences
to one interconnect feature. FSDP capacity remains unmeasured.

* Executable Task Composition
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:example
:END:
The following CPU-tested example constructs a symmetric contrastive task
with Matryoshka dimensions and differentiates it using ordinary Equinox
transformations. The projection and synthetic pairs make the interface
self-contained; they are not a benchmark. New tasks can instead implement
the scalar-loss interface, and representation-aware tasks can expose the
additional boundary used by compatible modifiers and execution paths.

#+NAME: example:task-composition
#+LATEX: \begin{minipage}{\linewidth}\small
#+begin_src python
import equinox as eqx
import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp
from representax.models import DenseEncoder
from representax.tasks import build_task
from representax.tasks.modifiers import MatryoshkaModifierConfig
from representax.tasks.retrieval import (
    MNRConfig, RetrievalConfig, retrieval_batch,
)

model = DenseEncoder(8, 4, key=jax.random.key(0))
x = jnp.eye(8)
batch = retrieval_batch(
    query=x, document=x, positive_mask=jnp.eye(8, dtype=bool),
)
task = build_task(
    RetrievalConfig(), MNRConfig(symmetric=True),
    modifiers=(MatryoshkaModifierConfig(dimensions=(4, 2)),),
)
loss, gradients = eqx.filter_value_and_grad(
    lambda model: task.loss(model, batch).loss
)(model)

* Learning Signals and Extension Directions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:research-directions
:END:
Learning signals shape what an encoding is encouraged to retain, discard, or
predict. Table \ref{tab:learning-signals} maps the approaches motivating this
toolkit. These categories overlap: masked prediction can reconstruct an input,
a latent predictor can use a moving teacher, and supervised pairs can define
a contrastive objective. The table is a research map, not a taxonomy of
mutually exclusive algorithms or a claim that all listed routes are benchmarked.

#+begin_export latex
\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{Learning signals, intended representational effects, and current scope. Effects are motivations, not guaranteed properties of a learned model.}\label{tab:learning-signals}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{.15\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{.24\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{.27\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{.24\linewidth}@{}}\toprule
Approach & Training signal & What it encourages & Representax scope \\ \midrule
Supervised learning & Labels, relevance judgments, or preferences & Features useful for the specified task & Classification, ranking, and reward tasks; selected paired measurements. \\
Reconstruction & Recover an observation from its encoding & Information needed to recover the chosen target & Denoising text autoencoder implemented; not benchmarked here. \\
Contrastive learning & Relate selected positives and distinguish alternatives & Geometry reflecting the chosen relationships & Dense, token-level, and multimodal objectives; throughput and adaptation studies. \\
Masked prediction & Infer missing content from context & Dependencies within observations & ModernBERT MLM implemented and used for scaling; broader modality recipes remain open. \\

The implemented denoising task reconstructs clean token sequences through an
autoregressive decoder conditioned on an encoder representation. Offline
distillation tasks match teacher embeddings, score margins, or candidate-score
distributions. Those components are not evidence that image autoencoders,
diffusion training, arbitrary forecasting, or complete online self-distillation
systems have been implemented or evaluated. Extending the toolkit in those
directions requires the corresponding models, data transformations, and
task-appropriate evaluation, not merely another loss name.

World-model and latent-diffusion research
[cite:@ha2018worldmodels;@rombach2022latentdiffusion] motivates studying
representations as components of generative and decision-making systems.
Additional modalities, including olfactory measurements and asynchronous
sensor streams, motivate different encoders and alignment assumptions.
The transferable design idea is to expose those scientific decisions while
reusing data access, optimization, state management, and distributed execution.
Whether a proposed representation helps must be established experimentally.
